    Descent, torsion and rank

    The classical two-isogeny descent for curves \(y^2=x^3+bx\) supplies the Kummer map, its multiplicativity, the identification of its kernel with the image of an isogeny, and the factorization of doubling through the isogeny and its dual.

    Let \(K\) be a field of characteristic different from \(2\) and \(b\in K^\times\), and let \(E_b:y^2=x^3+bx\) (an elliptic curve: its discriminant is \(-64b^3\ne0\)). Write \(K^\times/K^{\times2}\) multiplicatively.

    • The Kummer map (the \(x\)-squareclass map) \(\alpha_b:E_b(K)\to K^\times/K^{\times2}\) is $\(\alpha_b(O)=1,\qquad \alpha_b\bigl((0,0)\bigr)=b\,K^{\times2},\qquad \alpha_b\bigl((x,y)\bigr)=x\,K^{\times2}\ \ (x\ne0).\)$
    • The two-isogeny \(\varphi_b:E_b\to E_{-4b}\) is $\(\varphi_b(O)=\varphi_b\bigl((0,0)\bigr)=O,\qquad \varphi_b\bigl((x,y)\bigr)=\Bigl(\frac{y^2}{x^2},\ \frac{y(b-x^2)}{x^2}\Bigr)\ \ (x\ne0).\)$ Its values lie on \(E_{-4b}:Y^2=X^3-4bX\), by the identity \(y^2(b-x^2)^2x^2=y^6-4bx^4y^2\) for \(y^2=x^3+bx\).
    • The rescaling \(\lambda_b:E_{16b}\to E_b\) is \(\lambda_b(O)=O\), \(\lambda_b((x,y))=(x/4,\,y/8)\); it is a group isomorphism (the change of coordinates \((x,y)\mapsto(u^2x,u^3y)\) with \(u=1/2\)), with inverse \((x,y)\mapsto(4x,8y)\).

    In this chapter \(b=-d^2\) gives \(E=E_{-d^2}\), \(E'=E_{4d^2}:y^2=x^3+4d^2x\), the isogeny \(\varphi=\varphi_{-d^2}:E\to E'\) and the dual isogeny \(\hat\varphi=\lambda_{-d^2}\circ\varphi_{4d^2}:E'\to E_{-16d^2}\to E\); and \(b=-\delta^2\) gives the curve \(C=E_{-\delta^2}\).

    Let \(K\) be a field of characteristic different from \(2\) and \(b\in K^\times\). The Kummer map \(\alpha_b:E_b(K)\to K^\times/K^{\times2}\) is a group homomorphism. In particular \(\alpha_b(2P)=1\) for every \(P\in E_b(K)\).

    Along the way: let \((x_1,y_1)\) and \((x_2,y_2)\) be affine points of \(E_b(K)\) that are not negatives of each other, let \(\lambda\) be the slope of the chord (or tangent) through them, and let \(x_3=\lambda^2-x_1-x_2\) be the abscissa of their sum. Then \(x_1x_2x_3=(y_1-\lambda x_1)^2\).

    Proof sketch. (Silverman, Proposition X.4.9; Silverman-Tate, Section III.5.) Since \(\alpha_b(-P)=\alpha_b(P)\) and squareclasses have exponent two, it suffices to show \(\alpha_b(P_1)\alpha_b(P_2)\alpha_b(P_3)=1\) whenever \(P_1+P_2+P_3=O\). If one of the points is \(O\) this is \(\alpha_b(-P)=\alpha_b(P)\). Otherwise the three points are the intersections, with multiplicity, of a line \(y=\lambda x+\nu\) with \(E_b\), so \(x^3+bx-(\lambda x+\nu)^2=(x-x_1)(x-x_2)(x-x_3)\) and \(x_1x_2x_3=\nu^2\). If no \(x_i\) is \(0\) this gives the claim (\(\nu\ne0\)). If some \(P_i=(0,0)\), then \(\nu=0\), the other two abscissas are the roots of \(x^2-\lambda^2x+b\), and their product is \(b=\alpha_b((0,0))\) modulo squares. The case analysis matches the chord, tangent and vertical cases of the addition law; in the formula for \(x(P+Q)\) via the slope, the identity follows from the factorization of the cubic above, with \(\nu=y_1-\lambda x_1\). Finally \(\alpha_b(2P)=\alpha_b(P)^2=1\), since square classes have exponent two.

    The descent index

    The point \(R=(1+s,2s)\) lives on the isomorphic curve \(C:y^2=x^3-\delta^2x\), \(\delta=3-s\), and its doubling, transported back, is \(P_0\). On \(E\) the Kummer image is generated by the classes of \(-1\), \(1+s\) and \(d\), of order eight; on the isogenous curve \(E':y^2=x^3+4d^2x\) it is trivial. Together these give the weak Mordell-Weil index.

    Let \(\epsilon=1+s\), \(\delta=3-s\) and \(C=E_{-\delta^2}:y^2=x^3-\delta^2x\) over \(K_0\). Then \(\epsilon^4=17+12s\), \(\epsilon^6=99+70s\), \(\delta=\epsilon^4d\), and the map $\(\theta:E(K_0)\to C(K_0),\qquad \theta(O)=O,\quad \theta(x,y)=(\epsilon^4x,\ \epsilon^6y)\)$ is an isomorphism of groups, with inverse \((x,y)\mapsto(\epsilon^{-4}x,\epsilon^{-6}y)\).     Proof sketch. \((75-53s)(17+12s)=3-s\) and \(\epsilon^6=\epsilon^2\epsilon^4=(3+2s)(17+12s)\). For \(y^2=x^3-d^2x\), multiplying by \(\epsilon^{12}\) gives \((\epsilon^6y)^2=(\epsilon^4x)^3-\epsilon^8d^2(\epsilon^4x)\) and \(\epsilon^8d^2=\delta^2\); the converse is the same computation, \(\epsilon\) being a unit. The map preserves the group law because chord and tangent slopes are multiplied by \(\epsilon^2\) (for the tangent slope use \(3(\epsilon^4x)^2-\delta^2=\epsilon^8(3x^2-d^2)\)), so the sum formulas \(x_3=\lambda^2-x_1-x_2\), \(y_3=\lambda(x_1-x_3)-y_1\) scale by \(\epsilon^4\) and \(\epsilon^6\); vertical pairs go to vertical pairs. Two-torsion points have \(y=0\) and abscissas \(0,\pm d\), sent to \(0,\pm\delta\). Abscissas are multiplied by the square \(\epsilon^4\), and \((0,0)\) has class \(-d^2\) on \(E\) and \(-\delta^2\) on \(C\), both equal to \(-1\) modulo squares.

    Proof sketch. (Silverman, Theorem VIII.3.1 and Section VIII.6.)
