    Forests and minors over \(\mathbb F_2\)

    Over \(\mathbb F_2\) a determinant is a permanent, and the minors of the Laplacian of a family of bits count forests. This section proves the counting statements that the identity \(T_{5b}(n)=\det M_{5b}\) of row 5b rests on. No arithmetic enters: \(V\) is any finite set (for an odd number \(n\), the set of its primes), the bits \(a_{ij}\in\mathbb F_2\) are arbitrary, and the Laplacian \(L_U\) of the bits on \(U\subseteq V\) is defined here; for the bits of \(n\), \(L_V\) is Smith's matrix \(L\).

    There are three main results. The forest lemma, the case over \(\mathbb F_2\) of the all-minors matrix-tree theorem, writes a minor of \(L_U\) as a sum over forests. The second expands the determinant of the symmetric block matrix \(P(c,d)=\begin{pmatrix}D_d&L^{\mathsf T}\\L&D_c\end{pmatrix}\) as a forest sum. The third relates the forests on \(V\) to the set partitions of \(V\) whose blocks are unions of trees.

    Let \(V\) be a finite set and \(M\) a \(V\times V\) matrix over \(\mathbb F_2\). For \(R,C\subseteq V\) put $\(\operatorname{per}M[R,C]=\sum_{\sigma}\prod_{i\in R}M_{i\sigma(i)},\)$ the sum over the bijections \(\sigma:R\to C\). This is the minor of \(M\) with rows \(R\) and columns \(C\) used below; it needs no signs and no ordering of \(R\) and \(C\). Then:

    1. (principal minors) for \(S\subseteq V\), \(\det M[S,S]=\operatorname{per}M[S,S]\); in particular \(\det M=\operatorname{per}M[V,V]\);
    2. if \(|R|\ne|C|\), then \(\operatorname{per}M[R,C]=0\);
    3. \(\operatorname{per}M[R,C]\) depends only on the rows of \(M\) indexed by \(R\);
    4. (transpose) \(\operatorname{per}M^{\mathsf T}[A,C]=\operatorname{per}M[C,A]\) for \(A,C\subseteq V\).

    Proof sketch. (1) In the Leibniz expansion of \(\det M[S,S]\) every sign \(\pm1\) is \(1\) in \(\mathbb F_2\), and the permutations of \(S\) are exactly the bijections \(S\to S\); the case \(S=V\) is \(\det M\). (2) There are no bijections. (3) The products only involve rows in \(R\). (4) Inverting a bijection \(A\to C\) gives a bijection \(C\to A\), and this matches the two sums term by term: with \(c=\sigma(a)\), \(\prod_{a\in A}M^{\mathsf T}_{a\sigma(a)}=\prod_{c\in C}M_{c\sigma^{-1}(c)}\).

    Let \(V\) be a finite set, \(M\) a \(V\times V\) matrix over \(\mathbb F_2\), \(R,C\subseteq V\), and let \(\operatorname{per}M[R,C]\) be the minor of \(M\) with rows \(R\) and columns \(C\), the sum over the bijections \(R\to C\). Then:

    1. (row expansion) for \(i\in R\), \(\operatorname{per}M[R,C]=\sum_{v\in C}M_{iv}\operatorname{per}M[R\setminus\{i\},C\setminus\{v\}]\);
    2. (linearity in a row) for \(i\in R\) and finitely many row vectors \(r_u\), the minor of the matrix \(M\) with row \(i\) replaced by \(\sum_u r_u\) is the sum over \(u\) of the minors of \(M\) with row \(i\) replaced by \(r_u\); in particular this holds for a sum \(r+s\) of two rows;
    3. (equal rows) if \(i\ne k\) lie in \(R\) and rows \(i\) and \(k\) of \(M\) are equal, then \(\operatorname{per}M[R,C]=0\).

    Proof sketch. (1) Group the bijections by \(v=\sigma(i)\): removing \(i\) leaves a bijection \(R\setminus\{i\}\to C\setminus\{v\}\), and each such bijection extends uniquely by \(i\mapsto v\); a bijection is onto \(C\), so every \(v\in C\) occurs. (2) By (1), whose terms are linear in row \(i\), while the smaller minors in (1) do not involve row \(i\), since a minor depends only on the rows it uses. (3) Composing with the transposition of \(i\) and \(k\) is an involution of the bijections \(R\to C\); it has no fixed point, since \(\sigma(i)\ne\sigma(k)\), and it keeps each product, since rows \(i\) and \(k\) agree. So the terms cancel in pairs.

    Let \(K\) be a field, \(I\) a finite index set, \(A\) an \(I\times I\) matrix over \(K\) and \(u,w\in K^I\). Write \(A^{(j\leftarrow u)}\) for \(A\) with column \(j\) replaced by \(u\). Then $\(\det(A+uw^{\mathsf T})=\det A+\sum_{j\in I}w_j\det A^{(j\leftarrow u)}.\)$ If moreover \(Ax=u\) for some \(x\in K^I\), then $\(\det(A+uw^{\mathsf T})=\det A\,(1+w\cdot x).\)$ No invertibility is assumed. The determinant is additive in a column over finite sums: if column \(j\) of \(A\) is replaced by \(\sum_{u\in T}f_u\), the determinant is \(\sum_{u\in T}\det A^{(j\leftarrow f_u)}\).

    Over \(\mathbb F_2\), where \(-t=t\), \(s-t=s+t\) and \(t^2=t\):

    • for an \(I\times I\) matrix \(A\) and \(v\in\mathbb F_2^I\), the bordered matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}A&v\\v^{\mathsf T}&0\end{pmatrix}\), indexed by \(I\) and one more index, has determinant \(\det(A+vv^{\mathsf T})+\det A\);
    • for a symmetric \(I\times I\) matrix \(B\) and \(v\in\mathbb F_2^I\), \(\sum_{i,j}v_iv_jB_{ij}=\sum_iv_iB_{ii}\).

    Proof sketch. Transpose: row \(i\) of \(A^{\mathsf T}+wu^{\mathsf T}\) is row \(i\) of \(A^{\mathsf T}\) plus \(w_iu^{\mathsf T}\). By multilinearity in the rows, the determinant is the sum, over the sets \(s\) of rows, of the determinant of the matrix whose rows in \(s\) are those of \(A^{\mathsf T}\) and whose other rows are \(w_iu^{\mathsf T}\). If two rows lie outside \(s\) the term vanishes: the two rows are multiples of \(u^{\mathsf T}\), so one of them is zero or a multiple of the other. What remains is \(s=I\), giving \(\det A\), and \(s=I\setminus\{j\}\), giving \(w_j\det A^{(j\leftarrow u)}\). If \(Ax=u\), then \(u=\sum_kx_k(\text{column }k)\); by linearity in column \(j\), and since the terms with \(k\ne j\) have two equal columns, \(\det A^{(j\leftarrow u)}=x_j\det A\), which gives the second formula. For the bordered matrix, the matrix with corner \(1\) in place of \(0\) has determinant \(\det(A-vv^{\mathsf T})= \det(A+vv^{\mathsf T})\), by the Schur complement of the corner. Its last row is the last row of the bordered matrix plus \((0,\dots,0,1)\), so by linearity in the last row it is also the bordered determinant plus the determinant of a block triangular matrix with diagonal blocks \(A\) and \(1\), that is, plus \(\det A\). For the quadratic form, the terms \((i,j)\) and \((j,i)\) with \(i\ne j\) are equal by symmetry and cancel, and \(v_i^2=v_i\).

    Forests and the forest lemma

    Expanding the diagonal entries of the Laplacian turns a minor into a sum over maps. A map with a cycle has an even number of compatible bijections and drops out, and each forest contributes once exactly when the deleted columns meet every tree once.

    Let \(V\) be a finite set and \(a=(a_{ij})_{i,j\in V}\) a family of bits \(a_{ij}\in\mathbb F_2\).     Let \(V\) be a finite set with \(N\) elements and \(f:V\to V\) a map.

    • The root of \(i\in V\) is \(r_f(i)=f^N(i)\), and \(f\) is acyclic if every root is a fixed point of \(f\).
    • For \(U,R\subseteq V\), \(f\) moves \(R\) in \(U\) if it is the identity outside \(R\) and, for \(i\in R\), \(f(i)\in U\) and \(f(i)\ne i\).
    • For \(U,F\subseteq V\), a forest on \(U\) rooted at \(F\) is an acyclic map that moves \(U\setminus F\) in \(U\). Its tree at \(\rho\in F\) is \(T_\rho=\{v\in U:r_f(v)=\rho\}\), and a set \(W\) is a transversal of it if, for every \(\rho\in F\), exactly one element of \(W\) has root \(\rho\).
    • Given bits \(a_{ij}\in\mathbb F_2\) (\(i,j\in V\)), the weight of \(f\) on \(R\subseteq V\) is \(\mathrm{wt}_R(f)=\prod_{i\in R}a_{if(i)}\); for a forest on \(U\) rooted at \(F\) we write \(\mathrm{wt}(f)=\mathrm{wt}_{U\setminus F}(f)\).
    • The roots of \(g\) in \(U\) are its fixed points in \(U\), and a forest on \(U\) is a forest on \(U\) rooted at some \(F\subseteq U\).

    These notions satisfy the following.

    1. In an acyclic map every periodic point is fixed, and a map that is not acyclic has a periodic point that is not fixed. So \(f\) is acyclic exactly when it has no cycle of length at least \(2\).
    2. A fixed point is its own root. If \(f\) is acyclic, \(f(x)\) and every \(f^k(x)\) have the same root as \(x\).
    3. Let \(f\) move \(U\setminus F\) in \(U\). Then a point \(x\) with \(f(x)\ne x\) lies in \(U\setminus F\), a point outside \(U\setminus F\) is its own root, and the iterates and the root of a point of \(U\) stay in \(U\). If moreover \(f\) is acyclic, the root of every point of \(U\) lies in \(F\).
    4. A map is a forest on \(U\) if and only if it is the identity outside \(U\), maps \(U\) into \(U\) and is acyclic. A forest on \(U\) rooted at \(F\subseteq U\) has exactly \(F\) as its roots in \(U\); so every forest on \(U\) is rooted at exactly one \(F\subseteq U\), namely its set of roots in \(U\).
    5. Hence, for every \(\mathbb F_2\)-valued function \(H\) of a set and a map, $\(\sum_{F\subseteq U}\ \sum_{f}H(F,f)=\sum_{g}H\bigl(R_U(g),g\bigr),\)$ where \(f\) runs over the forests on \(U\) rooted at \(F\), \(g\) over the forests on \(U\), and \(R_U(g)\) is the set of roots of \(g\) in \(U\).

    A pairing of a finite set \(X\) is a fixed-point-free involution of \(X\): a map \(s:X\to X\) with \(s(x)\ne x\) and \(s(s(x))=x\) for all \(x\in X\), that is, a partition of \(X\) into pairs. The number of pairings of \(X\) is odd if \(|X|\) is even and even if \(|X|\) is odd. For distinct \(x,y\in X\), the pairings \(s\) of \(X\) with \(s(x)=y\) correspond bijectively to the pairings of \(X\setminus\{x,y\}\).

    Proof sketch. The bijection removes the pair \(\{x,y\}\) from a pairing with \(s(x)=y\), and puts it back in a pairing of \(X\setminus\{x,y\}\). The parity statement is by strong induction on \(|X|\). The empty set has one pairing and a point has none. For \(|X|\ge2\) choose distinct \(x,y\in X\) and conjugate pairings by the transposition \(\tau\) of \(x\) and \(y\), \(s\mapsto\tau s\tau\). This is an involution of the pairings of \(X\), and its fixed points are exactly the pairings with \(s(x)=y\). The other pairings cancel in pairs, so the number of pairings of \(X\) has the parity of the number of pairings of \(X\setminus\{x,y\}\), a set of size \(|X|-2\), and induction applies.
