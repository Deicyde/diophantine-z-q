    Mordell–Weil by two-isogeny descent

    For a positive integer \(n\) let \(C_n:y^2=x^3-n^2x\) and \(C'_n:y^2=x^3+4n^2x\) over \(\mathbb Q\). When \(\mathrm{Sel}_2(C_n)\) has order \(8\), a point of infinite order on \(C_n\) yields every 2-Selmer class of \(C_n\), but only once \(C_n(\mathbb Q)\) is known to be finitely generated. This section proves that by the classical route. The two-isogeny descent shows that \(2C_n(\mathbb Q)\) has finite index, and the descent theorem with the naive height turns the finite index into finite generation, as for the generator curve in Mordell–Weil for \(E/K_0\) from the descent.

    The descent uses the Kummer map \(\alpha_b\), the two-isogeny \(\varphi_b:E_b\to E_{-4b}\) and the rescaling \(\lambda_b:E_{16b}\to E_b\) of the descent chapter, for the curves \(E_b:y^2=x^3+bx\); here \(C_n=E_{-n^2}\) and \(C'_n=E_{4n^2}\). Over the number field \(K_0\) the index \([E(K_0):2E(K_0)]\) was computed exactly. Here only its finiteness is needed, and it follows from the finiteness of the two Kummer images over \(\mathbb Q\).

    This section writes \(n\) for the positive integer of the curve; the rest of the positive-rank chapter, following the Proposition 0 chapter, writes \(d\) and \(C_d\) for the same curves, and the statement of finite generation is also given in that form.

    Two-torsion

    \(C_n\) has full rational two-torsion, of order four. The two-torsion lemma of the 2-descent proves the same count over any field in which \(2\ne0\), as a separate statement.

    Let \(n\) be a positive integer and \(C_n:y^2=x^3-n^2x\) over \(\mathbb Q\). Then \(C_n\) is an elliptic curve, and for each \(e\in\{0,n,-n\}\) the point \((e,0)\) is a nonsingular point of \(C_n\). A point \(P\in C_n(\mathbb Q)\) satisfies \(2P=O\) if and only if $\(P\in\{O,\ (0,0),\ (n,0),\ (-n,0)\}.\)$ In particular exactly \(4\) points \(P\in C_n(\mathbb Q)\) satisfy \(2P=O\).

    Proof sketch. \(C_n\) is the curve \(E_b:y^2=x^3+bx\) with \(b=-n^2\ne0\); its discriminant \(-64b^3=64n^6\) is nonzero, so \(C_n\) is elliptic. Each \(e\in\{0,\pm n\}\) is a root of \(x^3-n^2x=x(x-n)(x+n)\), so \((e,0)\) lies on \(C_n\), and points of an elliptic curve are nonsingular. Clearly \(2O=O\). For an affine point \(P=(x,y)\) we have \(-P=(x,-y)\), so \(2P=O\) if and only if \(y=0\); then \(x(x-n)(x+n)=0\), that is \(x\in\{0,n,-n\}\), and conversely. The four points are distinct: \(O\) is not affine, and \(0,n,-n\) are distinct because \(n\ne0\).

    The two-isogeny is a homomorphism

    The finite-index argument needs \(\varphi_b\) to be additive. Collinear points with nonzero abscissas go to collinear points whose abscissas sum to the square of the new slope, and on \(E_{-4b}\) this forces the images to add up. The point at infinity, the point \((0,0)\) and the degenerate sums are treated separately.

    Let \(K\) be a field and \(b,\ell,\nu\in K\) with \(\nu\ne0\). Put $\(L=\frac b\nu-\ell,\qquad N=-\nu-\frac{b\ell^2}{\nu}.\)$

    1. (The image line.) If \(x\ne0\), \(y=\ell x+\nu\) and \(y^2=x^3+bx\), then \(X=y^2/x^2\) and \(Y=y(b-x^2)/x^2\), the formulas of the two-isogeny \(\varphi_b\) at \((x,y)\), satisfy \(Y=LX+N\).
    2. (The abscissa sum.) If \(x_1,x_2,x_3\in K\) are nonzero and $\(x_1+x_2+x_3=\ell^2,\qquad x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_2x_3=b-2\ell\nu,\qquad x_1x_2x_3=\nu^2,\)$ then $\(\sum_{i=1}^3\frac{(\ell x_i+\nu)^2}{x_i^2}=L^2 .\)$
    3. (Tangency.) If in 1 also \(2y\ell=3x^2+b\), then \(2LY=3X^2-4b\).

    No assumption on the characteristic of \(K\) or on \(b\) is needed.

    Proof sketch. Each identity becomes polynomial after clearing the nonzero denominators \(x\) (or \(x_1,x_2,x_3\)) and \(\nu\). In 1, substitute \(y=\ell x+\nu\): the difference of the two sides is a multiple of \(y^2-x^3-bx\). In 2, use the first two relations to eliminate \(x_3=\ell^2-x_1-x_2\) and \(b=x_1x_2+(x_1+x_2)x_3+2\ell\nu\); the difference is then a polynomial multiple of \(x_1x_2x_3-\nu^2\). In 3, use the tangency condition to eliminate \(b=2y\ell-3x^2\); the difference is a polynomial multiple of the curve equation.

    Let \(K\) be a field of characteristic different from \(2\), \(b\in K^\times\), and let \(\varphi_b:E_b(K)\to E_{-4b}(K)\) be the two-isogeny. Write \(T=(0,0)\).

    • (Doubling.) If \(P=(x,y)\) has \(x\ne0\), \(y\ne0\) and \(2P\) has nonzero abscissa, then \(\varphi_b(2P)=2\varphi_b(P)\). Equivalently, \(\varphi_b(2R)=2\varphi_b(R)\) whenever \(R\notin\{O,T\}\) and \(2R\notin\{O,T\}\).

    Proof sketch. (Silverman–Tate, Section III.4.) Chords. Let \(y=\ell x+\nu\) be the chord through \(P_1,P_2\). Then \(P_1+P_2=(x_3,-(\ell x_3+\nu))\) with \(x_1+x_2+x_3=\ell^2\). Subtracting the curve equations of \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) and dividing by \(x_1-x_2\) gives \(x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_2x_3=b-2\ell\nu\). By the image-line identity, \(\varphi_b(P_1)\), \(\varphi_b(P_2)\) and \(\varphi_b(-(P_1+P_2))\) lie on \(Y=LX+N\), so \(\varphi_b(P_1+P_2)=(X_3,-(LX_3+N))\). By the abscissa-sum identity, \(X_1+X_2+X_3=L^2\). Finally \(X_1\ne X_2\): by the curve equations, \(X_1=X_2\) would give \(x_1x_2(x_1-x_2)(x_1x_2-b)=0\). Addition along a line on \(E_{-4b}\) gives the claim.

    Doubling. Take the tangent slope \(\ell\), with \(2y\ell=3x^2+b\), and the same relations with \(x_1=x_2=x\). From \(x(2P)\cdot(2y)^2=(x^2-b)^2\) and \(x(2P)\ne0\) we get \(b\ne x^2\), so the image ordinate \(y(b-x^2)/x^2\) is nonzero.     The index of \(2C_n(\mathbb Q)\)

    Over any field of characteristic different from \(2\), the dual isogeny factors doubling, so \(2E_b(K)\) has finite index once both Kummer images are finite. Over \(\mathbb Q\) the Kummer image of \(E_c\) lies in the classes of the signed divisors of \(c\), by a count of \(p\)-adic valuations. Applied to \(C_n\) and \(C'_n\), this gives the weak Mordell–Weil theorem.

    Let \(c\) be a nonzero integer. Then the Kummer image \(\alpha_c(E_c(\mathbb Q))\subseteq\mathbb Q^\times/\mathbb Q^{\times2}\) of \(E_c:y^2=x^3+cx\) is finite.

    Along the way:

    • if \(x,y\in\mathbb Q\) satisfy \(y^2=x^3+cx\) and \(x\ne0\), then \(v_p(x)\) is even for every prime \(p\nmid c\);
    • if \(B\) is a positive integer and \(q\in\mathbb Q^\times\) has \(v_p(q)\) even for every prime \(p\nmid B\), then \(q\equiv m\) modulo squares for some integer \(m\) with \(|m|\) dividing \(B\).

    Proof sketch. (Silverman–Tate, Section III.5.)

    Valuations. Let \(p\nmid c\), so \(v_p(cx)=v_p(x)\). If \(y=0\) then \(x^2=-c\), so \(2v_p(x)=v_p(c)=0\). Otherwise \(2v_p(y)=v_p(x^3+cx)\). If \(v_p(x)<0\), the term \(x^3\) dominates: \(2v_p(y)=3v_p(x)\), so \(v_p(x)\) is even. If \(v_p(x)>0\), the term \(cx\) dominates: \(2v_p(y)=v_p(x)\).

    Signed divisors. Write \(q=r/s\) in lowest terms with \(s>0\), let \(N=rs=qs^2\), and write \(|N|=t^2a\) with \(a\) squarefree. Put \(m=\operatorname{sign}(N)\,a\), so \(q\equiv N\equiv m\) modulo squares. Every prime \(p\mid a\) divides \(B\): otherwise \(v_p(N)=v_p(q)+2v_p(s)\) is even, so \(v_p(a)=v_p(N)-2v_p(t)\ge1\) is even, hence \(p^2\mid a\), against squarefreeness. As \(a\) is squarefree, \(|m|=a\) divides \(B\).

    Finiteness. The image lies in the classes of the integers \(m\) with \(|m|\) dividing \(|c|\), a finite set. Indeed \(\alpha_c(O)=1\) (take \(m=1\)) and \(\alpha_c((0,y))=c\) (take \(m=c\)). For \(x\ne0\), \(\alpha_c((x,y))\) is the class of \(x\), which by the two lemmas, with \(B=|c|\), is the class of such an \(m\).

    Let \(n\) be a positive integer. The group \(C_n(\mathbb Q)\) of rational points of \(C_n:y^2=x^3-n^2x\) is finitely generated.

    In the form used by the positive-rank chapter: for every natural number \(d\ne0\), the group \(C_d(\mathbb Q)\) is finitely generated.

    Proof sketch. (Silverman, Theorem VIII.3.1 and Section VIII.6; Silverman–Tate, Chapter III.) \(C_n\) is an elliptic curve over \(\mathbb Q\). Let \(h\) be the naive height: \(h(O)=0\), and \(h((x,y))=\sum_v\log\max(1,|x|_v)\) over the places of \(\mathbb Q\), which is \(\log\max(|r|,|s|)\) for \(x=r/s\) in lowest terms. Then:

    • \(h\ge0\);
    • there is a constant \(c\) with \(|h(P+Q)+h(P-Q)-2h(P)-2h(Q)|\le c\) for all \(P,Q\) (the approximate parallelogram law);
    • for every bound \(B\) only finitely many \(P\) have \(h(P)\le B\) (Northcott's property for \(\mathbb Q\), since each abscissa determines at most two points).

    The descent theorem says that an abelian group \(G\) with such a height, in which \(2G\) has finite index, is finitely generated. By the weak Mordell–Weil theorem, \(2C_n(\mathbb Q)\) has finite index in \(C_n(\mathbb Q)\). The second form is the first with \(n=d\).
