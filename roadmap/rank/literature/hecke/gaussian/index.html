    The Hecke character \(\psi_d\) and its coefficients

    This subsection is about the Gaussian integers, with one exception: the vanishing of \(\lambda_d(p)\) at the primes \(p\mid2d\) is read off from Deuring's identity at those primes. For a natural number \(d\), the Hecke character \(\psi_d\) is \(\psi_d(\alpha)=\left(\frac{N(\alpha)}d\right)u(\alpha)\,\alpha\) on \(\mathbb Z[i]\), where \(u(\alpha)\) is a unit read off \(\alpha\) modulo \(4\) (the primary unit, \(0\) when \(1+i\) divides \(\alpha\)). It is completely multiplicative and takes the value \(1\) on the four units, so the coefficients \(\lambda_d(n)=\frac14\sum_{N(\alpha)=n}\psi_d(\alpha)\) are a sum over the ideals of norm \(n\). For squarefree \(d\equiv1\pmod4\) its coefficients are those of \(C_d\) (Deuring's identity).

    These coefficients are multiplicative, by counting the factorizations \(\gamma=\alpha\beta\) with \(N(\alpha)=m\), \(N(\beta)=n\) for coprime \(m,n\). At a prime \(p\equiv1\pmod4\) that does not divide \(d\), the factorization \(p=\pi\bar\pi\) into two non-associate Gaussian primes gives the Hecke relation \(\lambda_d(p^{k+2})=\lambda_d(p)\lambda_d(p^{k+1})-p\lambda_d(p^k)\); at a prime \(p\equiv3\pmod4\) not dividing \(d\), the elements of norm \(p^{k+2}\) are \(p\) times those of norm \(p^k\), which gives \(\lambda_d(p^{k+2})=-p\lambda_d(p^k)\); and at \(2\) and at the primes dividing \(d\) the coefficients of the prime powers vanish. Together, for odd squarefree \(d\), the coefficients are those of an Euler product with local factors \((1-\lambda_d(p)T+pT^2)^{-1}\) at \(p\nmid2d\) and \(1\) at \(p\mid2d\).

    Throughout, a pair \((a,b)\in\mathbb Z^2\) is also the Gaussian integer \(a+bi\), with norm \(N(a,b)=a^2+b^2\); \(A_n\) is the set of Gaussian integers of norm \(n\); and \(\left(\frac an\right)\) is the Jacobi symbol as in the Hecke character.

    A pair \((a,b)\in\mathbb Z^2\) stands for the Gaussian integer \(a+bi\in\mathbb Z[i]\). The product of pairs is \((a,b)(c,e)=(ac-be,\,ae+bc)\), the conjugate of \((a,b)\) is \((a,-b)\), and the norm is \(N(a,b)=a^2+b^2\).     1. A pair lies in \(A_n\) exactly when its norm is \(n\). \(U\) has four elements and \(A_1=U\).
    2. The map \((a,b)\mapsto a+bi\) from \(\mathbb Z^2\) to \(\mathbb Z[i]\) is a bijection; it carries the product of pairs to the product, the conjugate to the complex conjugate, and \(N(a,b)\) to the norm of \(a+bi\).

    4. For \(e\in U\): \(e\bar e=1\), \(\bar e\in U\), \(\bar{\bar e}=e\) and \(N(e)=1\).

    Let \(m,n>0\) be coprime natural numbers. Pairs and Gaussian integers are identified as in Gaussian integers as pairs, and \(\gcd\) in \(\mathbb Z[i]\) is the greatest common divisor computed by the Euclidean algorithm.

    1. If \(\gamma\in\mathbb Z[i]\) has norm \(mn\), then \(N(\gcd(\gamma,m))=m\).
    2. For every \(y\in A_{mn}\) there are exactly four pairs \((\alpha,\beta)\in A_m\times A_n\) with \(\alpha\beta=y\).

    Proof sketch. (1) Let \(D=\gcd(\gamma,m)\) and \(E=\gcd(\gamma,n)\). Since \(D\) divides \(m\) and \(\gamma\), \(N(D)\) divides \(m^2\) and \(mn\), hence \(m\) by coprimality; likewise \(N(E)\) divides \(n\). Writing \(D\) and \(E\) as \(\mathbb Z[i]\)-combinations of \(\gamma,m\) and of \(\gamma,n\) and using \(mn=\gamma\bar\gamma\) shows \(\gamma\mid DE\), so \(mn\mid N(D)N(E)\). With \(N(D)\le m\) and \(N(E)\le n\) this forces \(N(D)N(E)=mn\); if \(N(D)<m\), then \(N(D)N(E)<mn\).

    (2) Let \(D=\gcd(y,m)\), so \(N(D)=m\) by (1), and \(Q=y/D\), so \(N(Q)=n\). For each unit \(e\), the pair \((eD,\bar eQ)\) is a solution, and distinct units give distinct solutions since \(D\ne0\). Conversely, if \(\alpha\beta=y\) with \(N(\alpha)=m\), then \(\alpha\mid m\) (as \(\alpha\bar\alpha=m\)) and \(\alpha\mid y\), so \(D=\alpha c\) with \(N(c)=1\): \(c\) is a unit, \(\alpha=D\bar c\) and \(\beta=cQ\).

    Notation of Gaussian integers as pairs: \(A_n\) is the set of Gaussian integers of norm \(n\).

    1. If \(n\equiv3\pmod4\), then \(A_n\) is empty.
    2. Let \(p\equiv3\pmod4\) be prime. If \(p\mid a^2+b^2\) for integers \(a,b\), then \(p\mid a\) and \(p\mid b\). For every \(k\ge0\), \(A_{p^{k+2}}=\{p\beta:\beta\in A_{p^k}\}\).
    3. A Gaussian integer whose norm is a prime number is a prime element of \(\mathbb Z[i]\).
    4. Let \(p>0\) be a natural number, \(z\) a Gaussian integer of norm \(p\), \(n\) a natural number and \(f:\mathbb Z^2\to\mathbb C\). Then $\(\sum_{\beta\in A_n}f(z\beta)=\sum_{\gamma\in A_{pn},\ z\mid\gamma}f(\gamma).\)$

    Proof sketch. (1) Squares are \(0\) or \(1\) modulo \(4\), so \(a^2+b^2\not\equiv3\).

    (2) If \(p\nmid b\), then \((ab^{-1})^2\equiv-1\pmod p\), but \(-1\) is not a square modulo a prime \(p\equiv3\pmod4\). So \(p\mid b\), and then \(p\mid a\). For \(\gamma\in A_{p^{k+2}}\) both coordinates are divisible by \(p\), and \(\gamma/p\in A_{p^k}\); conversely \(N(p\beta)=p^2N(\beta)\).

    (3) A unit has norm \(1\), so a Gaussian integer \(x\) of prime norm is not a unit; if \(x=yz\), then \(N(y)N(z)\) is prime, so \(y\) or \(z\) has norm \(1\) and is a unit. So \(x\) is irreducible, hence prime, as \(\mathbb Z[i]\) is a principal ideal domain.

    (4) \(z\ne0\). The map \(\beta\mapsto z\beta\) sends \(A_n\) into the elements of \(A_{pn}\) divisible by \(z\), with inverse \(\gamma\mapsto\gamma/z\).

    For an integer \(a\) and a natural number \(n\), \(\left(\frac an\right)\) is the Jacobi symbol: the product of the Legendre symbols \(\left(\frac ap\right)\) over the prime factors \(p\) of \(n\) counted with multiplicity, where \(\left(\frac a2\right)\) is \(1\) for odd \(a\) and \(0\) for even \(a\), and the empty product (for \(n=0,1\)) is \(1\). Let \(d\) be a natural number and \(u\) the primary unit. Gaussian integers are pairs as in Gaussian integers as pairs. The Hecke character \(\psi_d\) is the function on \(\mathbb Z[i]\) $\(\psi_d(\alpha)=\left(\frac{N(\alpha)}{d}\right)u(\alpha)\,\alpha .\)$ Its finite part is the function \(\omega_d\) on \((\mathbb Z/4d)^2\) $\(\omega_d(x)=\left(\frac{\tilde x_1^2+\tilde x_2^2}{d}\right)u(\tilde x_1\bmod4,\ \tilde x_2\bmod4),\)$ where \(\tilde x_1,\tilde x_2\in\{0,\dots,4d-1\}\) are the least residues of the coordinates of \(x\).

    1. If \(d\ne0\), then \(\omega_d(\alpha\bmod4d)=\left(\frac{N(\alpha)}{d}\right)u(\alpha)\) for every \(\alpha\in\mathbb Z^2\); so \(\psi_d(\alpha)=\omega_d(\alpha\bmod4d)\,\alpha\).
    2. \(\psi_d(\alpha\beta)=\psi_d(\alpha)\,\psi_d(\beta)\) for all \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb Z[i]\).
    3. \(\psi_d(1)=\psi_d(-1)=\psi_d(i)=\psi_d(-i)=1\). Hence \(\psi_d(i\alpha)=\psi_d(-\alpha)=\psi_d(\alpha)\), and \(\psi_d(e\alpha)=\psi_d(\alpha)\) for every unit \(e\).
    4. If \(p\equiv1\pmod4\) is prime and \(p\nmid d\), then \(\psi_d(p)=p\).

    Proof sketch. (1) The Jacobi symbol depends on its numerator modulo \(d\), and \(u\) on residues modulo \(4\); both are determined modulo \(4d\).

    (2) The norm is multiplicative, the Jacobi symbol is multiplicative in its numerator, \(u\) is multiplicative (primary unit (1), (2)), and so is \(\alpha\mapsto\alpha\).

    (3) \(\left(\frac1d\right)=1\), and the table gives \(u(1)=1\), \(u(-1)=-1\), \(u(i)=-i\), \(u(-i)=i\), so \(u(e)e=1\) for each unit. The rest follows from (2).

    (4) \(\left(\frac{p^2}{d}\right)=1\) because \(p\) is coprime to \(d\), and \(u(p)=u(1,0)=1\) because \(p\equiv1\pmod4\).
