    The Fourier identity for the Hecke character

    The functional equation of a periodic twist needs the finite Fourier transform \(\hat c\) of the coefficient function \(c\) to be supported on \((1+i)\mathbb Z[i]\) and to equal a constant multiple of \(c\) there. For the finite part \(\omega_d\) of the Hecke character, on \((\mathbb Z/4d)^2\), this subsection proves it with the constant \(4d(1+i)\left(\frac2d\right)\) for every odd squarefree \(d\equiv1\pmod4\).

    The Chinese remainder theorem splits \(\hat\omega_d\) into a 2-part, a sum over \((\mathbb Z/4)^2\) of the primary unit against fourth roots of unity, and an odd part, a sum over \((\mathbb Z/d)^2\) of the Jacobi symbol of the norm against \(d\)-th roots of unity. The 2-part is a finite table: \(4(1+i)u(\mu)\) at \((1+i)\mu\), and \(0\) off \((1+i)\mathbb Z[i]\). The odd part is multiplicative in \(d\), and at an odd prime \(p\) it is the two-dimensional quadratic Gauss sum \(\sum_{x,y}\chi(x^2+y^2)e(xn_1+yn_2)=p\,\chi(n_1^2+n_2^2)\), which follows from the one-variable quadratic Gauss sum by completing the square.

    Throughout, \(\left(\frac an\right)\) is the Jacobi symbol as in the Hecke character, and \(N(\nu)=\nu_1^2+\nu_2^2\).

    For \(\nu\in\mathbb Z^2\) and a natural number \(d\) define the 2-part and the odd part $\(G_2(\nu)=\sum_{a\in\{0,1,2,3\}^2}u(a)\,e^{2\pi i(a_1\nu_1+a_2\nu_2)/4},\qquad G_d(\nu)=\sum_{b\in\{0,\dots,d-1\}^2}\left(\frac{b_1^2+b_2^2}d\right)e^{2\pi i(b_1\nu_1+b_2\nu_2)/d},\)$ with \(u\) the primary unit. Let \(\omega_d\) be the finite part of the Hecke character on \((\mathbb Z/4d)^2\) and \(\hat\omega_d\) its Fourier transform as in the functional equation of a periodic twist, with \(M=4d\).

    1. If \(d\) is odd, the map \((a,b)\mapsto(da+4b)\bmod4d\) is injective on \(\{0,1,2,3\}\times\{0,\dots,d-1\}\).
    2. If \(d\) is odd and \(g:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb C\) satisfies \(g(x)=g(x\bmod4d)\) for every \(x\), then \(\sum_{j=0}^{4d-1}g(j)=\sum_{a=0}^{3}\sum_{b=0}^{d-1}g(da+4b)\).
    3. \(e^{2\pi in}=1\) for every integer \(n\). If \(M\ne0\), then \(e^{2\pi i(A+kMn)/M}=e^{2\pi iA/M}\) for \(A\in\mathbb C\) and integers \(k,n\). For an integer \(x\) and a natural number \(M\), \(x\bmod M=x-qM\), where \(q\) and \(x\bmod M\) are the quotient and the remainder of Euclidean division of \(x\) by \(M\) (\(q=0\) and \(x\bmod M=x\) when \(M=0\)).
    4. If \(d\equiv1\pmod4\), then \(\hat\omega_d(\nu)=G_2(\nu)\,G_d(\nu)\) for every \(\nu\in\mathbb Z^2\).

    Proof sketch. (1) \(4\) and \(d\) are coprime (Chinese remainder theorem). (2) By (1) and counting, the map of (1) is a bijection onto \(\{0,\dots,4d-1\}\). (3) Direct.

    (4) The summand of \(\hat\omega_d(\nu)\) is \(4d\)-periodic in each coordinate (by (3)), so (2) may be applied in both coordinates: \(j=da+4b\). Then \(N(j)\equiv16N(b)\pmod d\) and \(\left(\frac{16}d\right)=1\), so \(\left(\frac{N(j)}d\right)=\left(\frac{N(b)}d\right)\); \(j\equiv da\equiv a\pmod4\) as \(d\equiv1\pmod4\), so \(u(j)=u(a)\); and \((da+4b)\cdot\nu/(4d)=a\cdot\nu/4+b\cdot\nu/d\). The double sum factors.

    Let \(p\) be a prime, \(\chi\) the quadratic character of \(\mathbb F_p\) with complex values (\(\chi(0)=0\)), \(e(t)=e^{2\pi i\tilde t/p}\) for \(t\in\mathbb F_p\) with \(\tilde t\) any integer representing \(t\), and \(g=\sum_{t\in\mathbb F_p}\chi(t)e(t)\) the quadratic Gauss sum.

    1. \(\chi\) is quadratic (every value of \(\chi\) is \(0\), \(1\) or \(-1\)), and \(\chi\) is not the trivial character if \(p\ne2\). \(\chi(0)=0\); \(\chi(t)^2=1\) for \(t\ne0\); \(\chi(w^{-1})=\chi(w)\) for every \(w\). For every integer \(m\), the Jacobi symbol \(\left(\frac mp\right)\) is \(\chi(m\bmod p)\).
    2. If \(p\ne2\), then \(\sum_t\chi(t)e(tm)=\chi(m)\,g\) for every \(m\in\mathbb F_p\).
    3. If \(p\ne2\) and \(t\ne0\), then \(\sum_xe(tx^2)=\chi(t)\,g\).
    4. If \(p\ne2\) and \(t\ne0\), then \(\sum_xe(tx^2+nx)=e\bigl(-n^2/(4t)\bigr)\sum_xe(tx^2)\) for every \(n\in\mathbb F_p\).
    5. If \(p\ne2\), then \(g^2=\chi(-1)\,p\) and \(g\ne0\).
    6. (The two-dimensional quadratic Gauss sum.) If \(p\ne2\), then for all \(n_1,n_2\in\mathbb F_p\) $\(\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb F_p}\chi(x^2+y^2)\,e(xn_1+yn_2)=p\,\chi(n_1^2+n_2^2).\)$

    Proof sketch. (1) Properties of the quadratic character; for \(w\ne0\), \(\chi(w^{-1})\chi(w)=1\) and \(\chi(w)^2=1\). For prime \(p\) the Jacobi symbol is the Legendre symbol.

    (2) For \(m\ne0\), the Gauss sum of \(\chi\) with the additive character \(t\mapsto e(tm)\) is \(\chi(m)^{-1}g\), and \(\chi(m)^{-1}=\chi(m)\); for \(m=0\) the sum \(\sum_t\chi(t)\) vanishes as \(\chi\) is nontrivial.

    (3) The number of \(x\) with \(x^2=s\) is \(1+\chi(s)\), so \(\sum_xe(tx^2)=\sum_s(1+\chi(s))e(ts)\); the sum of \(e(ts)\) over \(s\) is \(0\), and (2) evaluates the rest.

    (4) Complete the square: \(tx^2+nx=t\bigl(x+n/(2t)\bigr)^2-n^2/(4t)\), and shift \(x\).

    (5) The standard evaluation of the square of a quadratic Gauss sum; \(g\ne0\) because \(\chi(-1)p\ne0\).

    (6) By (2) and (5), \(\chi(m)=g^{-1}\sum_t\chi(t)e(tm)\) for every \(m\). Insert this for \(m=x^2+y^2\), exchange the sums, and separate the variables. For \(t\ne0\) each inner sum is evaluated by (4) and (3), giving \(\chi(t)^2g^2e\bigl(-(n_1^2+n_2^2)/(4t)\bigr)\); the term \(t=0\) vanishes. Substitute \(w=-1/(4t)\), use (2) once more, and simplify with (5) and \(\chi(-1)^2=1\).

    Let \(G_d\) be the odd part of the factorization. The odd Gauss sum identity holds for a natural number \(d\) if $\(G_d(\nu)=d\left(\frac{\nu_1^2+\nu_2^2}d\right)\qquad\text{for every }\nu\in\mathbb Z^2.\)$

    1. The identity holds for every odd prime \(p\), and for \(d=1\).
    2. Let \(m,n\) be coprime. The map \((a,b)\mapsto(na+mb)\bmod mn\) is injective on \(\{0,\dots,m-1\}\times\{0,\dots,n-1\}\). If moreover \(m,n>0\) and \(g:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb C\) satisfies \(g(x)=g(x\bmod mn)\) for every \(x\), then \(\sum_{j=0}^{mn-1}g(j)=\sum_{a=0}^{m-1}\sum_{b=0}^{n-1}g(na+mb)\).
    3. If \(m,n>0\) are coprime and the identity holds for \(m\) and for \(n\), it holds for \(mn\).
    4. If the identity holds for every odd prime, it holds for every odd squarefree \(d\).

    Proof sketch. (1) For \(d=p\), identify \(\{0,\dots,p-1\}^2\) with \(\mathbb F_p^2\); the Jacobi symbols become the quadratic character \(\chi\) of \(\mathbb F_p\), and the identity is the two-dimensional quadratic Gauss sum (quadratic Gauss sums (6), with (1) there). For \(d=1\) both sides are \(1\).

    (2) The Chinese remainder theorem, and a bijection by counting.

    (3) Apply (2) in both coordinates: \(b=n b'+m b''\). Then \(e^{2\pi i\,b\cdot\nu/(mn)}= e^{2\pi i\,b'\cdot\nu/m}e^{2\pi i\,b''\cdot\nu/n}\); \(N(b)\equiv n^2N(b')\pmod m\) with \(\left(\frac{n^2}m\right)=1\), and likewise modulo \(n\); and the Jacobi symbol is multiplicative in its denominator. So \(G_{mn}(\nu)=G_m(\nu)G_n(\nu)\), and the right sides multiply in the same way.

    (4) Induction over the factorization of \(d\): a squarefree prime power is a prime, odd because \(d\) is; \(d=1\) is (1); a product of coprime factors is (3); \(d=0\) is not odd.
